# A structured taxonomy of real interrogation techniques for AI simulation
 
**The most effective police interview techniques fall into two philosophical camps — information-gathering and accusatorial — and an AI detective agent can draw tactically from both.** The PEACE model and Cognitive Interview represent the evidence-based, non-coercive end of the spectrum, proven as effective at eliciting confessions from guilty suspects while dramatically reducing false confession risk. The Reid Technique represents the accusatorial tradition, still dominant in U.S. law enforcement but increasingly abandoned internationally. Between these poles sits a rich toolkit of micro-techniques — Strategic Use of Evidence, cognitive load imposition, anchoring, bait questions — that can be deployed as discrete tactical moves based on conversation state. What follows is a complete breakdown of each framework and technique, structured for implementation as selectable tactics in an AI-driven interrogation simulation.
 
---
 
## The PEACE model operates as a five-phase state machine
 
Developed in the UK between 1990–1993 by psychologists Tom Williamson, Eric Shepherd, and Ray Bull in response to wrongful convictions (the Birmingham Six, Guildford Four), PEACE replaced the word "interrogation" with "investigative interviewing." It is now mandatory across England, Wales, and Northern Ireland and has been adopted by Australia, New Zealand, Norway, Canada, and others. The UN's Méndez Principles on Effective Interviewing are modeled on it.
 
**Phase 1 — Planning and Preparation** is considered the most important phase. The interviewer reviews all case material, distinguishes established facts from conjecture, and creates a written interview plan containing: offences under investigation with points to prove, topic boxes (discrete subject areas to probe sequentially), an evidence inventory ranked by strength, pre-planned challenges with evidence withheld for strategic disclosure, and contingency plans for "no comment" interviews. The interviewer assesses the interviewee's vulnerability, cultural background, and prior police contact. Critically, the interviewer decides which evidence to reveal and which to withhold — this directly enables the SUE technique within the Challenge sub-phase.
 
**Phase 2 — Engage and Explain** has two components. The Engage sub-phase builds rapport through neutral conversation, active listening (verbal encouragers, open posture, eye contact), and explicit transfer of control: "You saw what happened, not I — I expect you to tell me what happened." The Explain sub-phase sets ground rules: the interview's purpose, recording procedures, the formal caution, and explicit instructions including "If you don't understand a question, say so," "Don't try to fill in gaps," and "If I summarize incorrectly, please correct me." This phase trains the interviewee to give elaborated responses.
 
**Phase 3 — Account, Clarification, and Challenge** is the substantive heart. It contains three sequential sub-phases that must proceed in order:
 
**Sub-phase 3a (Free Recall)** opens with a single open question — "Tell me everything about what happened on [date]" — and the suspect speaks entirely uninterrupted. The interviewer uses active listening only, mentally noting topics, gaps, and inconsistencies for later probing. This produces information that research shows is "invariably accurate."
 
**Sub-phase 3b (Probing/Clarification)** systematically selects topics from the free recall and probes each using the **funnel questioning sequence**: TED questions ("Tell me… / Explain… / Describe…") → 5WH questions (Who/What/When/Where/Why/How) → closed probing questions → then opening back out. Each topic is summarized before moving to the next. The goal is generating **checkable facts and provable lies** — the more detail obtained, the more verifiable or falsifiable the account becomes.
 
**Sub-phase 3c (Challenge)** occurs only after both the suspect's agenda and the investigator's agenda are complete. The interviewer presents contradictions one at a time — internal inconsistencies within the suspect's account, or external inconsistencies between the account and known evidence — framed as neutral observations, never accusations: "You said you were at home at 8pm, but we have CCTV footage showing you at [location] at 8:15pm. Can you help me understand that?" Evidence is introduced strategically, calibrated for maximum impact against the committed account. The tone remains calm and controlled; no raised voice, no threats, no minimization. This differs fundamentally from Reid's confrontation: PEACE challenges seek explanation, not confession. A suspect who has already committed to a detailed false account cannot easily backtrack when confronted with contradictory evidence they didn't know the interviewer possessed.
 
**Phase 4 — Closure** summarizes everything using the interviewee's own words, invites corrections and additions, explains next steps, and conducts a welfare check. **Phase 5 — Evaluate** assesses the information obtained, its impact on the investigation, and the interviewer's own performance. Research by Milne and Bull found evaluation is the most neglected phase.

## Cognitive Interview techniques exploit four memory retrieval pathways
 
Ronald Fisher and Edward Geiselman developed the Cognitive Interview in 1984, funded by the U.S. Department of Justice. It rests on two principles from cognitive psychology: Tulving and Thomson's **encoding specificity principle** (retrieval is most effective when context at recall matches context at encoding) and the **multicomponent view of memory traces** (memories form networks with multiple retrieval pathways).
 
**Context Reinstatement** is the foundational technique. The interviewer instructs the witness to mentally reconstruct three dimensions before attempting recall: environmental context (physical setting, lighting, temperature, sounds, smells, spatial layout), emotional context (feelings during the event), and cognitive context (what they were thinking about, prior activities). Specific instructions include: "Close your eyes and think back to that day. What could you see? What was the temperature? How were you feeling?" This is especially effective when substantial time has elapsed. Eye closure reduces visual interference and enhances concentration.
 
**Report Everything** explicitly removes the witness's self-editing filter. The instruction — "Tell me everything you remember, even things that seem small or unimportant" — is not permission to guess; it's paired with "Don't guess" and "Say 'I don't know' when uncertain." The rationale is that seemingly trivial details act as associative cues that trigger recall of additional, more important information. Each retrieved detail becomes a new retrieval cue.
 
**Change Temporal Order** asks the witness to recall events in reverse chronological order after an initial forward narrative. This serves three functions: it disrupts schema-based recall (preventing witnesses from filling gaps with "how things usually happen"), it activates different retrieval pathways by approaching the event from a different temporal direction, and — critically for deception detection — it makes fabrication substantially harder. Vrij et al. (2008) demonstrated that liars find reverse recall far more difficult because they've rehearsed stories in forward order. Liars in reverse-recall conditions needed more prompts, made larger temporal leaps, and frequently reverted to forward order. Police observers' detection accuracy improved from **42% to 60%** when suspects told stories in reverse order.
 
**Change Perspective** asks the witness to describe events from another person's viewpoint. It is the least effective of the four mnemonics — Milne and Bull (2002) found it was the only one that did not significantly facilitate accurate recall — and carries confabulation risk. Many modern protocols omit it, especially with children and vulnerable witnesses.
 
The **Enhanced Cognitive Interview** (Fisher and Geiselman, 1992) added social and communicative components that substantially improve effectiveness. **Transfer of control** explicitly makes the witness the expert: "You saw what happened, not I." **Witness-compatible questioning** tailors questions to the witness's unique mental images rather than following a checklist — if the witness is currently thinking about the perpetrator's smell, questions about the knife are deferred. **Focused retrieval** uses eye closure and slow pacing to deepen concentration.
 
Meta-analyses confirm the CI's power. Köhnken et al. (1999), synthesizing 42 studies with ~2,500 participants, found the CI produced **34% more correct information** with accuracy rates virtually identical to standard interviews (**85% vs. 82%**). Memon et al. (2010) replicated these findings across 65 experiments. Field studies with Miami robbery detectives showed CI-trained officers elicited **47% more information** with 94% corroboration accuracy.

## Twelve micro-techniques form a tactical toolkit for the AI agent
 
These techniques can be deployed as discrete moves based on conversation state. Each has a defined trigger condition, mechanism, and expected outcome.
 
**Strategic Use of Evidence (SUE)**, developed by Granhag, Hartwig, and Strömwall at the University of Gothenburg, is the most empirically validated micro-technique. It exploits the asymmetry between guilty and innocent suspects' strategies: guilty suspects try to assess what evidence the interviewer holds and develop avoidance strategies, while truth-tellers openly provide detailed accounts. The procedure has four phases: pre-interview evidence assessment (ranking evidence by strength and anticipating innocent explanations), rapport-building contact, a strategic questioning phase where the suspect's full account is elicited via open-ended questions without revealing any evidence, and evidence disclosure via the **Evidence Framing Matrix** — introducing evidence incrementally at three specificity levels: vague ("Some information has come to our attention"), moderate ("We have information about your whereabouts that evening"), and precise ("We have surveillance footage showing you at 1401 Airport Rd at 8pm"). SUE-trained investigators achieved **85.4% detection accuracy** versus 56.1% for untrained investigators.
 
**Baseline establishment and deviation detection** involves cataloging the interviewee's normal verbal and behavioral patterns during non-threatening biographical questions, then monitoring for "hot spots" when sensitive topics arise. Tracked patterns include speech rate, pause frequency, detail richness, hedging language, posture shifts, and micro-expressions. However, research is unambiguous about its limitations: DePaulo et al.'s 2003 meta-analysis found no single behavioral cue reliably indicates deception. Bond and DePaulo (2006) showed police officers perform no better than chance at behavioral lie detection. Baseline deviations should flag topics for further probing, never serve as evidence of deception.
 
**Assumptive questions** presuppose facts not yet established: "When you went back to the house that night, what happened?" rather than "Did you go back?" The psychological mechanism is that people tend to respond to a question's frame rather than challenge its presupposition. The Wicklander-Zulawski method structures these with follow-up exaggeration questions: after the subject denies an exaggerated claim ("It wasn't ten times, was it?"), their denial of the exaggeration implicitly concedes something lesser.
 
**Forced choice / alternative questions** constrain responses to options that both assume guilt: "Did you plan this out or did it just happen on the spur of the moment?" The suspect is driven to refute the morally worse option by accepting the less damaging one. This is Step 7 of the Reid Technique and represents the pivot point where a suspect makes a first admission.
 
**Minimization** downplays offense seriousness through sympathy, moral excuses, and normalization ("Anyone might have done the same thing under that pressure"). **Maximization** emphasizes evidence strength and consequence severity. Kassin and McNall (1991) demonstrated that minimization lowered sentencing expectations to the same degree as explicit promises of leniency — making it a legally permissible but psychologically equivalent substitute. Critically, Russano et al. (2005) found both tactics increase true and false confession rates, reducing their diagnostic value.
 
**Theme development** (Reid Step 2) offers the suspect a structured moral narrative — a face-saving story they can adopt to make confession psychologically safe. Common themes include blaming the victim, blaming circumstances, framing the act as accidental, or appealing to external pressures. The interrogator delivers this as a sympathetic monologue, observing which themes resonate. Theme development mirrors Sykes and Matza's (1957) neutralization theory — criminals already use internal justifications; the interrogator mirrors these back.
 
**Strategic silence** exploits the finding that silence becomes uncomfortable after just **4 seconds** (Koudenburg et al., 2010). After asking a critical question, the interviewer stops talking completely, maintains steady eye contact, and waits. Practitioners report effective silences of 5 minutes or more. The suspect can only resolve the escalating discomfort by speaking — often producing admissions, deflections, or denials that reveal information.
 
**Topic switching** disrupts rehearsed narratives by abruptly changing subjects, then returning to the original topic to catch inconsistencies. Liars rehearse linear narratives and rely on a "repeat strategy" for consistency; topic switching forces them to interrupt their sequence, manage a new topic, and then recall exactly what they previously said — a task much harder when the rehearsed narrative has been disrupted. Truth-tellers, drawing on actual memory, pick up where they left off more easily.
 
**Reverse chronology questioning**, used as an interrogation tactic rather than a CI memory aid, imposes cognitive load specifically on fabricators. Vrij et al. (2008) found deceptive suspects in reverse-recall conditions displayed more pauses, speech errors, decreased detail, and requests for prompts. However, Brimbal et al. (2025) failed to replicate some findings, and the technique may also burden truth-tellers recalling distant events.
 
**Cognitive load techniques** encompass any method that imposes additional processing demands: requesting very specific spatial details, asking suspects to draw diagrams while narrating, maintaining uninterrupted eye contact during responses, posing unanticipated questions ("What did you see on your left as you walked in?"), or imposing time pressure. The theory, championed by **Aldert Vrij**, holds that liars already operate near cognitive capacity (fabricating stories, suppressing truth, monitoring demeanor, tracking consistency) and have fewer resources for additional demands.
 
**Anchoring / commitment to details** gets the interviewee to commit to specific facts early — times, locations, people present — documented explicitly. The interviewer moves to other topics, then returns to the anchored details using different phrasing. Any changes, omissions, or additions create leverage. This technique is integral to both the PEACE Challenge phase and the SUE framework: early commitment creates the benchmark against which evidence is later measured.
 
**Bait questions** imply evidence may exist without confirming it: "Is there any reason why your fingerprints would be found on that safe?" The guilty suspect must pause to evaluate whether the evidence could exist, creating observable behavioral changes and potential story revision. Innocent suspects deny emphatically without hesitation. However, Luke, Strange, and Crozier (2017) demonstrated that bait questions create misinformation effects — jurors exposed to them falsely believe the hypothetical evidence is real.

## The Reid Technique's nine steps follow a guilt-presumptive escalation
 
Developed by John E. Reid and Fred Inbau in the 1940s–1950s and published in *Criminal Interrogation and Confessions* (1962, now in its 5th edition), the Reid Technique has trained over **500,000 professionals** and remains dominant in U.S. law enforcement. It consists of three phases: Factual Analysis, the Behavioral Analysis Interview (BAI), and the Nine Steps of Interrogation.
 
The **Behavioral Analysis Interview** is a non-accusatory, 30–40 minute structured interview using **behavior-provoking questions** designed to elicit different responses from truthful versus deceptive subjects. Key BAI questions include the Punishment Question ("What do you think should happen to the person who did this?" — deceptive subjects allegedly suggest lenient punishment), the Suspicion Question ("Who do you think did this?"), the Think Question ("Did you ever think about doing this, even though you didn't?"), and the Bait Question ("Is there any reason why a surveillance tape would show you in the area?"). Reid claims truthful subjects give direct, spontaneous, specific denials with strong language, while deceptive subjects give passive, guarded, short responses with qualifying phrases like "to the best of my memory."
 
The nine interrogation steps proceed as a deliberate escalation. **Step 1 (Direct Positive Confrontation)** opens with an unequivocal guilt statement: "Joe, the results of our investigation clearly indicate that you are the person who [committed the offense]." **Step 2 (Theme Development)** shifts to a sympathetic monologue offering moral justifications. **Step 3 (Handling Denials)** actively interrupts and discourages denials — the interrogator does not allow the suspect to voice denials, as this would strengthen the suspect's resolve. **Step 4 (Overcoming Objections)** reframes the suspect's logic-based reasons for innocence as partial admissions incorporated back into the theme. **Step 5 (Retaining Attention)** uses role reversal scenarios and physical gestures of camaraderie. **Step 6 (Handling Passive Mood)** recognizes surrender signs — head in hands, tears — and seizes the opportunity to transition. **Step 7 (Alternative Question)** presents the forced choice: "Did you plan this or did it just happen?" — either answer is an admission. **Step 8 (Oral Details)** expands the initial admission into a fact-rich confession with corroborating details. **Step 9 (Written Confession)** documents everything, sometimes including deliberate errors for the suspect to correct, establishing ownership of the statement.
 
The technique faces serious, well-documented criticisms. Of **311 DNA exonerees**, more than 25% had given false confessions, virtually all involving Reid-style tactics. Kassin and Kiechel's (1996) landmark Alt-key experiment demonstrated innocent participants would sign false confessions when presented with fabricated evidence. The guilt-presumptive structure creates confirmation bias: Kassin, Goldstein, and Savitsky (2003) showed that investigators who presume guilt conduct more aggressive interrogations, which produce behaviors they interpret as confirming guilt. Multiple countries have abandoned Reid-style methods — the UK replaced it with PEACE, Canada's RCMP adopted a PEACE-influenced model in 2015, and Germany's criminal procedure code bans deception and intimidation outright.

## Two-interviewer dynamics require precise role definition and coordination signals
 
The classic good-cop/bad-cop dynamic — formally called "Mutt and Jeff" in U.S. Army doctrine — has largely declined in professional practice. David Simon described it as "melodrama" that has "worn thin," and research shows good-cop tactics alone yield **3–5 times as much information** as the combined dynamic. Approximately 60% of suspects begin interrogations with cooperative intentions, and harsh tactics destroy that goodwill.
 
Modern professional tandem interviewing, studied most rigorously by Tkacukova and Oxburgh in their analysis of 20 interviews with suspected sex offenders, operates on a planned division of labor. The **lead interviewer conducts approximately 75% of questioning turns**; the second interviewer contributes roughly 25%. Roles are preplanned during the Planning and Preparation phase. The second interviewer's core functions include critical active listening, note-taking (freeing the lead from distraction), quality assurance of communication (catching misunderstandings), asking clarifying questions at natural topic transitions, covering preplanned evidence topics, and reinforcing institutional authority.
 
The second interviewer's participation nearly doubles during the Account, Clarify, and Challenge phase (22% of turns) compared to Engage and Explain (12%). Floor-passing is coordinated primarily through verbal signals — the lead asks "Got anything?" or "DC [name], any questions at this point?" — and through pre-planned topic-based handoffs. Effective teams show no pauses during handoffs. Problematic patterns include competing for the floor, discussing strategy in front of the suspect, and digressing to new topics before the lead has completed a line of questioning.
 
Strategic intervention by the second interviewer occurs at five trigger points: end of topic segments (the natural break between topics), when clarification is needed (the second interviewer detects ambiguity the lead missed), when specific evidence needs to be presented (assigned during planning), when rapport needs reinforcement (using collective "we" to express empathy), and when the interview dynamic is stalling (the other interviewer may establish rapport where the first could not).
 
Modern alternatives include the **ORBIT framework** (Observing Rapport-Based Interpersonal Techniques) from the University of Liverpool, which focuses on adaptive communication based on the subject's emotional state, and **Conversation Management** developed by Eric Shepherd, which has been embedded in UK policing for 40 years.

## Verbal deception research identifies content-based cues, not behavioral tells
 
The most robust finding in deception science is that humans are terrible at detecting lies. Bond and DePaulo's (2006) meta-analysis of 206 studies and **24,483 judges** found average accuracy of just **54%** — barely above chance. Truth detection was 61% accurate, but lie detection was only 47%, reflecting a strong truth bias. Trained law enforcement professionals performed no better than laypeople — they were simply more confident in their errors.
 
Verbal content analysis substantially outperforms behavioral observation. The most diagnostic single cue is **total amount of detail** — truth-tellers consistently provide more details than liars, endorsed by approximately 70% of deception research experts. DePaulo et al. (2003) found liars are less forthcoming, tell less compelling tales, include fewer ordinary imperfections (spontaneous corrections), and include fewer unusual or unexpected contents. However, the average effect size across all significant cues was only **d = 0.25** — small but real.
 
Truthful accounts characteristically contain spontaneous corrections ("No wait, it was Tuesday"), admissions of forgetting, rich sensory details (colors, sounds, textures, smells), unusual or unexpected details that would be difficult to fabricate, contextual embedding in time and place, reproduced dialogue, descriptions of the speaker's own mental state, self-deprecating admissions, accounts of others' mental states, and an unstructured production style that jumps around rather than following a polished chronological script.
 
Two formal frameworks operationalize these findings. **Criteria-Based Content Analysis (CBCA)**, formalized by Steller and Köhnken (1989) and grounded in Udo Undeutsch's hypothesis that experienced-based statements differ qualitatively from fabricated ones, specifies **19 criteria** organized in five categories: general characteristics (logical structure, unstructured production, quantity of details), specific contents (contextual embedding, descriptions of interactions, reproduction of conversation, unexpected complications), peculiarities of content (unusual details, superfluous details, accurately reported details misunderstood, related external associations, accounts of subjective mental state, attribution of perpetrator's mental state), motivation-related contents (spontaneous corrections, admitting lack of memory, raising doubts about own testimony, self-deprecation, pardoning the perpetrator), and offense-specific elements. Field studies show confirmed abuse cases scoring a mean of **24.8 versus 3.6** for doubtful cases, with no overlap in distributions. **Reality Monitoring (RM)**, based on Johnson and Raye's (1981) framework for distinguishing experienced from imagined events, predicts that truthful accounts contain more sensory/perceptual and contextual information while fabricated accounts contain more references to cognitive operations. Combined RM criteria correctly classified **86.6% of statements** when used within a Cognitive Interview for Suspects framework.
 
**Scientific Content Analysis (SCAN)**, developed by Avinoam Sapir, lacks empirical support. Bogaard et al. (2016) found it "was not able to correctly classify true and fabricated statements," and Vrij characterized it as lacking theoretical underpinning. Its continued use in multiple countries' law enforcement agencies represents a gap between practice and science.

## Conclusion: building the taxonomy for tactical selection
 
An AI detective agent operating this taxonomy would manage two layers of decision-making. The **structural layer** selects the interview framework — PEACE for information-gathering with cooperative or semi-cooperative subjects, a modified Reid-influenced approach for more resistant subjects in the game context. The **tactical layer** selects micro-techniques based on conversation state: SUE when evidence exists and the subject is providing their account, anchoring throughout to create commitment points, topic switching when detecting rehearsed narratives, reverse chronology to impose cognitive load on suspected fabricators, strategic silence after key confrontational moments, and bait questions to probe reactions to hypothetical evidence.
 
The middleware's deception-flagging system should prioritize CBCA and RM criteria over behavioral cues: monitoring for quantity of detail, presence or absence of spontaneous corrections, sensory richness, contextual embedding, unusual details, and unstructured production. These verbal content markers have effect sizes and classification accuracies far exceeding any behavioral observation approach. The system should flag statements that are too smooth, too chronological, lack sensory grounding, contain no self-corrections or uncertainty markers, and provide minimal peripheral detail — these are the most reliable indicators that a narrative has been constructed rather than recalled.
 
The two-interviewer dynamic maps naturally to an AI system where one agent conducts questioning while a parallel process monitors for gaps, tracks committed details against evidence, and identifies optimal intervention points. The research shows the second interviewer should intervene at topic transitions, when clarification is needed, and when the primary dynamic stalls — all states that can be detected programmatically from conversation structure.
